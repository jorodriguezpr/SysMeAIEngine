★ Start here: your first document assistant in 5 steps

Goal: ask questions in Chat and get answers taken from your documents, with the sources listed, all running on this computer with no cloud service needed. One Train from Folder job does the heavy lifting; you then connect the result to an answering model and start asking.

1Put your documents in one folder

  • Supported: PDF, Word (.docx), PowerPoint (.pptx), Excel (.xlsx), .txt.
  • Scanned PDFs and images (.png/.jpg/.tif) work when the OCR card on Train from Folder says on.
  • Subfolders are fine (they're scanned recursively).
  • Start small: 1–10 documents is plenty for a first try.

2Run a Train from Folder job

  • Source folder: the folder from step 1.
  • Job / model name: a short name, e.g. my-docs. Your search index gets this name.
  • Chunking: paragraph (the cleanest pieces; see the playground).
  • Leave the rest at the defaults (gpt2, lm, sft, 1 epoch).
  • Click Preview dataset size, then Start.
✓ The job appears in the Jobs table; open its log to watch progress.

3Wait, then check the search

  • The search index is built at the end of the job. Fine-tuning takes most of the time (about an hour at 2000 rows with gpt2 on this hardware). Lower Max rows for a quicker test.
  • Optional: in Evaluate a RAG corpus, pick your corpus, keep hybrid, and run it. It reports how often the right passage is found.
✓ Status turns done and your corpus appears in the corpus dropdowns.

4Connect the index to the answering model

  • Open Models.
  • Find the local answering model (the engine default is the Qwen 2.5 7B model, e.g. qwen7b-cpp or qwen7b-local).
  • In its RAG corpus column pick your job name and click Set.
✓ Why the 7B model and not the one you just trained? See the measured comparison below.

5Ask questions in Chat

  • On Settings make sure Grounded mode = Grounded (retrieve from documents first).
  • Ask in Chat. Answers list the passages they came from.
  • A question your documents don't cover should get a polite refusal. That means it's working, not guessing.
✓ Expect roughly 15–40 seconds per answer on CPU.

Search vs. fine-tuning: which one actually answers questions?

There are two ways to make a model "know" your documents. Fine-tuning changes the model's weights so it memorizes something of your text. Retrieval (RAG) leaves the model alone and, for every question, finds the relevant passages and hands them to the model to read. This project measured both on the same 30 questions about the same book:

Use retrieval (the search index) to answer questions. It scales to many documents, lists its sources, refuses when the answer isn't there (100% of out-of-document questions in testing), and gets better with a stronger answering model. No retraining is needed when you add documents; just run a new job.
Use fine-tuning to change style or vocabulary, not to store facts. A small model fine-tuned on a book still misspells names and mixes up details (see sections 6 and 8). It's a great way to learn how training works, and the Weights page shows exactly what changed.

Preparing your documents: a checklist

  • Text, not just pictures of text. If you can select text in the PDF, it has a text layer. Scans need OCR (check the OCR card).
  • One topic per folder / job. Mixing unrelated subjects makes retrieval pick passages from the wrong book.
  • Remove junk: duplicate copies, blank pages, tables of contents, and indexes add noise to search results.
  • Real paragraphs. Paragraph chunking relies on blank lines between paragraphs. Text exported with a line break on every line chunks worse.
  • Readable file names (e.g. chapter-03-genesis.pdf). The source name is shown next to every answer.
  • Spanish and English both work. The search embeddings are multilingual, and refusals come back in the question's language.
  • Start small, then grow. Test with a few files, check answers in Chat, then point a new job at the full folder.
  • Write down 10 test questions whose answers you know. Ask them after every change, so you know whether it really got better.

1. What is an AI model?

A language model is a very large mathematical function. You give it some text, and it predicts what text is most likely to come next — one small piece at a time. It learned those predictions by reading huge amounts of existing text during "training," the same way you'd get a feel for how sentences usually continue after reading enough of them, except the model does this with math (billions of adjustable numbers) instead of intuition.

Everything else on this page — tokens, embeddings, layers, training, fine-tuning, RAG — is really just answering one question in more detail: how does a pile of numbers learn to predict text well, and how do we steer it toward our own documents and language?

2. Tokens: how text becomes numbers

A model can't read letters — it only does math on numbers. So before any text reaches the model, a tokenizer chops it into small pieces (words, parts of words, or punctuation) and looks each piece up in a fixed dictionary, turning it into a number called a token id. The word "unbelievable" might become three tokens ("un", "believ", "able") if the model has never seen that exact whole word often enough to earn its own entry.

See it live: the Chat page shows prompt/completion token counts on every response.

3. Embeddings: turning meaning into geometry

Once text is tokens (numbers), the model converts each token into a list of a few hundred numbers called a vector — its embedding. The trick that makes this useful: during training, the model arranges these vectors in space so that words with related meaning end up near each other. "King" and "queen" end up close together; "king" and "bicycle" end up far apart. This is also exactly how this engine finds relevant passages in your documents — it turns your question into a vector the same way, and looks for document chunks whose vectors are nearby, which is a much better test of "is this relevant?" than just checking for shared words.

See it live: the Models page lists the embedding model this engine uses for that.

4. Neural networks and weights

A neural network is a stack of layers, each one taking the previous layer's output, multiplying it by a big table of adjustable numbers (the layer's weights), and passing the result to the next layer. Modern language models use a design called a transformer, where each layer also has an "attention" step that lets every token look at every other token in the input to decide what's relevant to it before moving on — this is what lets a model connect "it" back to the right noun several sentences earlier.

Training a model just means: nudge those weight numbers, over and over, until the network's predictions get better. There's no other magic ingredient — a trained model and an untrained one have the exact same structure, just different numbers in those weight tables.

See it live: the Weights page shows the real weight tables of whatever model is currently loaded, including a graph of the network's layers colored by how much each layer's weights have moved.

5. How training actually works

Training repeats one loop, many times:

Each single pass through step 1-4 is one training step. Doing this for every example in your dataset once is one epoch. The batch size is how many examples get shown together before a single nudge — bigger batches make each nudge more stable but need more memory, and fewer nudges happen per epoch as a result.

A real, concrete number from this exact project: fine-tuning a small model on a 236-example dataset with the wrong settings produced only 2 real training steps for the entire run — nowhere near enough nudges to teach it anything. That's a genuine bug this project hit and fixed (it now automatically raises the step count for small datasets). The lesson generalizes: a model is only as trained as however many real nudges it actually got, not how much data you pointed at it.

See it live: the Train from Folder page runs this whole loop on your own documents and shows you the resulting loss.

6. Overfitting: why more training isn't always better

You'd assume training longer always makes a model better. It doesn't, and this project has a real, measured example. A small model was fine-tuned on the same small set of documents at 1, 3, and 10 epochs:

  • 1 epoch: answers were somewhat error-prone but varied and engaged with the actual source content.
  • 3 epochs: barely different — the loss number improved by about 1%.
  • 10 epochs: the model collapsed onto the exact same short, generic sentence for every question, regardless of what was actually asked.

That last outcome is overfitting: instead of learning general patterns from the documents, the model started memorizing a narrow, safe response that happened to score well on that one small training set, at the cost of actually engaging with new questions. It's the AI equivalent of a student memorizing yesterday's exact quiz answers instead of understanding the subject — looks fine on that quiz, falls apart on any real question. More training only helps up to a point, and that point depends heavily on how much data you actually have.

7. Fine-tuning and LoRA

Training a model completely from scratch needs enormous amounts of data and compute. Fine-tuning instead starts from an already-trained model and nudges it a bit further using your own, much smaller dataset — cheaper, and it keeps everything the model already knew.

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) makes fine-tuning even cheaper: instead of nudging all of a model's weights (hundreds of millions of numbers), it freezes the original model completely and trains a small set of extra numbers "alongside" a few chosen layers — in this project, typically well under 1% of the model's total parameters. Those extra numbers get combined with the original weights at inference time. It's much faster to train and produces a tiny extra file instead of a whole new copy of the model, at some cost in how much the model can actually absorb compared to fully retraining every weight.

See it live: pick Method = lora on Train from Folder (the default sft retrains every weight, which suits the small models offered there). The Weights page graph then shows which layers carry the LoRA adapter.

8. Training data quality: a real, measured finding

This project ran a long series of real experiments trying to fix one specific, persistent problem: a small fine-tuned model kept misspelling a name ("Nicodemo" as "Nicodmo"/"Nicodamo") and giving vague or inaccurate answers, no matter what. Each lever that seemed like it should help was tried and measured:

  • More training epochs (1 → 3 → 10): barely moved the loss number, and 10 epochs actually made answers worse (see Overfitting above).
  • More source documents (7 → 49 files, ~9x more data): richer RAG retrieval, but the same model still misspelled the name and got confused mixing multiple retrieved passages together.
  • Constraining the sampling (limiting the model to its more-likely next-word choices): a real, measurable fluency improvement, but the specific spelling tic survived even this.

What actually fixed it: instead of training on the raw document text (which is only ever OCR'd book pages, not clean writing) or a cheap heuristic ("treat a chunk's first sentence as the question, the rest as the answer" — frequently mismatched, nonsensical, or ungrammatical), a stronger cloud AI model was asked to read each document chunk and write one genuine, accurate question and answer about it. The exact same small model, fine-tuned on those cloud-written question/answer pairs instead, produced the name spelled correctly in every single test run — something none of the epoch, data-size, or sampling changes ever achieved — plus noticeably more detailed, accurate answers.

The lesson generalizes well beyond this one project: a small model's biggest limitation usually isn't how long you train it or how much raw data you throw at it — it's whether what you're training it on is actually clean, correct, well-formed text. This technique (using a stronger model to generate better training examples for a smaller one) is called distillation.

See it live: enable a cloud AI provider on Settings, then pick "cloud_qa" as the dataset format on Train from Folder — each document chunk then gets a real, cloud-written question/answer pair instead of the cheap heuristic (costs one API call per chunk). The cloud is used only while building the dataset; answering stays local.

9. RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation

A model's knowledge is frozen at training time and limited by how much it could actually memorize (see overfitting above — small models struggle to memorize much reliably). RAG works around this: instead of relying on the model to "remember" your documents, it searches your actual documents for relevant passages at the moment you ask a question, and pastes those passages directly into the model's prompt as reference material. The model still writes the answer, but now it's writing with your real text in front of it instead of from memory — which is why grounding answers in retrieval scales far better than trying to train a small model to memorize everything.

This engine's retrieval combines three techniques:

  • Lexical search (TF-IDF): finds passages sharing actual words with your question. Fast, but misses passages that mean the same thing using different words.
  • Semantic search (embeddings): finds passages with related meaning, even with no shared words at all — this is where the vectors from section 3 get used for real.
  • Reranking: after gathering candidates from both of the above, a second, more careful model reads the question and each candidate passage together and re-scores them, since checking a pair directly is more accurate than comparing two vectors from a distance.

See it live: build a searchable index of your own documents on Train from Folder, connect it on Models, and chat with it grounded (see Start here).

10. CPU vs GPU vs NPU

A model's real work is almost entirely large grids of numbers being multiplied together. A CPU does this with a handful of parallel lanes per core — flexible, but not built for this specific job. A GPU has thousands of small cores built specifically for doing huge numbers of these multiplications at once, which is why GPUs are typically an order of magnitude faster for both training and chat responses. An NPU (found in newer "AI PC" chips) is a further specialized chip for this same kind of math, but — at least on this project's hardware, verified directly rather than assumed — it's inference-only (it can never train a model) and its benefit only shows up under sustained, batched workloads; for one-off single questions, the overhead of using it can actually make things slower than the CPU.

Troubleshooting: common first-time problems

What you seeLikely cause → what to do
The job finishes almost instantly with very few rowsThe PDFs are scans without a text layer and OCR is off. Check the OCR card on Train from Folder, or export the documents as text.
Chat answers from general knowledge and ignores my documentsThe corpus isn't connected, or Grounded mode is off. Recheck steps 4 and 5 of Start here.
"Not in the documents" for a question that is coveredRetrieval missed the passage. Rephrase with words the document uses, check names are spelled as in the text, or run Evaluate a RAG corpus. Paragraph chunking helps.
Answers are cut short or incompleteThe engine already uses the complete answer style by default (setting local_prompt_style); if it was changed to a brief style, set it back. Measured: "be brief" answers looked wrong because they left out half the facts.
The model I trained writes gibberish or repeats one sentenceExpected for tiny models (minilm, gpt2) and for too many epochs (section 6). Use it to learn; use the 7B answering model plus retrieval for real answers.
Training would take many hoursLower Max rows (rows are sampled evenly across all files), keep gpt2, keep 1 epoch. Qwen 0.5B trains about 40x slower.
The job stops with a memory warningClose other programs, or lower Max rows / Max seq. A rough RAM check runs before each job.

11. Glossary (quick reference)

TokenA small chunk of text (word/part of a word) represented as a number.
EmbeddingA list of numbers representing a token or piece of text's meaning as a point in space.
WeightsThe adjustable numbers inside a model that get changed during training.
LossA single number measuring how wrong the model's predictions currently are — training tries to make this smaller.
EpochOne full pass through the entire training dataset.
StepOne single weight update, based on one batch of examples.
Batch sizeHow many examples are shown to the model before one weight update.
Fine-tuningContinuing to train an already-trained model on your own smaller dataset.
SFTSupervised fine-tuning: retraining all of a model's weights on your data (the default Method for the small models here).
LoRAA cheap fine-tuning method that trains a small add-on instead of the whole model.
OverfittingA model memorizing its training data too narrowly instead of learning general patterns.
RAGAnswering questions using passages retrieved from real documents instead of relying on memorized training data.
GroundedA Chat answer written from retrieved passages, with sources, that refuses when the documents don't cover the question.
ChunkA slice of a document, small enough to search and feed to a model as one piece.
CorpusA named collection of ingested documents with a built search index (named after the Train from Folder job).
OCROptical character recognition: reading the text out of scanned pages and images.
RerankingRe-scoring a shortlist of retrieved passages with a more careful (but slower) model.
QuantizationStoring a model's weights with less precision to save memory/speed, at some accuracy cost (the local 7B model uses 4-bit "Q4_K_M").
DistillationUsing a stronger AI model to generate better training examples for a smaller one — see section 8, a real fix found in this project's own testing.
